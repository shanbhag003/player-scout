    What is in here

      Coverage is what happened, not how. Every ball is here; line, length, pace and shot type are not, because that data is commercial and has no open equivalent.

      Men's T20 only for now. A player's Test and one-day profiles are different games and get scored separately.

      How these numbers are built

      Where it comes from

      Every ball of 22,905 matches, from Cricsheet. Each file carries a registry mapping names to stable player ids, so nothing here depends on matching people by name.

      Dates of birth and full names join from Wikidata on an exact ESPNcricinfo id. Which country a player represents is read from who they turn out for, not from a passport.

      How a profile is built

      Sixteen rates per batter, eleven per bowler, split by phase of the innings and by whether the bowler was pace or spin. Pace and spin are derived from the deliveries themselves, seeded with about fifty labels.

      Thin records are pulled toward the average for their role, because a strike rate off eighty balls is mostly noise. Old cricket counts for less, on a three-year half-life measured from a player's own last match.

      Levelling competitions

      One coefficient per metric per competition, fitted only from players seen in more than one. Comparing whole competitions would show which has the better players, not which is the harder place to play.

      It does almost nothing between competitions of similar standard and cuts prediction error by a quarter to two-fifths across the widest gaps, which is the case scouting cares about.

      How similar is measured

      Metrics are standardised within a role, compressed with principal component analysis, and similarity is the distance between two players across the axes that remain.

      Tested by splitting each player's matches in two and treating the halves as strangers. A top-order batter finds himself around 45th of 235, where guessing would put him 118th.
